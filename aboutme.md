I am a social and cultural historian of the French colonial world, working comparatively across Creole spaces in Africa’s Indian Ocean islands, North America, and the Caribbean.

My research and teaching tend to focus on places dismissed as marginal to the histories of empires, but which I see as especially revealing of the processes that made and sustained them. Much of my research asks how people in Réunion and Mauritius, French island colonies in the Indian Ocean, understood and experienced an Age of Revolutions most readily associated with the Atlantic. My first book traces the shifting politics of race on Réunion, showing how a colony often treated as peripheral to the French and Haitian Revolutions was deeply implicated in the racial transformations they set in motion. My work on the memoirs of a globetrotting Mauritian sailor explores what it meant to call oneself “Creole” in a maritime world remade by those revolutions. My latest book project documents the scope and scale of slaveholding by Catholic orders and congregations on both islands and across the wider French colonial empire. Beyond reconstructing the daily lives and resistance strategies of the thousands enslaved by missionaries in the latter third of the eighteenth century, the project examines their relationships with the clergy who enslaved them and traces what became of them when revolutionary governments declared them “national property.”

Doing history from the margins can help us rethink how the past figures in the stories we tell about our communities. Arkansas itself has often been treated as one such “marginal” space in broader histories of empire, despite having long formed part of a cosmopolitan Creole world that endured under French, Spanish, and U.S. rule. At the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, I try to cultivate my students’ curiosity about how the history of our region intersects with histories that reach far beyond it. Together, we use digital storytelling, spatial humanities, and public-facing history to investigate those connections—and to carry the questions they raise beyond the classroom.

I live and work in beautiful Central Arkansas. I grew up along the Piscataqua River in coastal New Hampshire, where I first learned the power of history to foster a meaningful sense of place.
