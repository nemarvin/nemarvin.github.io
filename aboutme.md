I am a social and cultural historian of the French colonial world, working comparatively across Creole spaces in Africa’s Indian Ocean islands, North America, and the Caribbean.

My research and teaching tend to focus on places dismissed as marginal to the histories of nations or empires, but which I see as especially revealing of broader historical processes. Much of my research asks how people in Réunion and Mauritius, French island colonies in the Indian Ocean, understood and experienced an Age of Revolutions often narrated from the North Atlantic. My first book project traces the shifting politics of race on Réunion during the era of the French and Haitian Revolutions. My work on the memoirs of a handful of globetrotting Mauritian sailors explores what it meant to call oneself “Creole” in a maritime world remade by those revolutions. My second book project documents the scope and scale of slaveholding by Catholic orders and congregations on both islands and across the wider French colonial empire. It asks, in particular, what happened to the thousands of people enslaved by missionaries when revolutionary governments declared them “national property.”

Doing history from the “margins” can help us rethink how the past figures in the stories we tell about our communities. In the broader histories of empires, Arkansas is one such “marginal” space. I am ever curious about the connections between the local and the global. Along with my students at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, I am constantly experimenting with digital storytelling, spatial humanities, and public-facing history to bring those connections—and the new questions they raise—to new audiences.

I live and work in beautiful Central Arkansas. But home is also the Piscataqua River region of coastal New Hampshire, where I first learned the power of history to foster a meaningful sense of place.
