I am a social and cultural historian of the French colonial world, working comparatively across Creole spaces in Africa’s Indian Ocean islands, North America, and the Caribbean.

My research and teaching tend to focus on places dismissed as marginal to the histories of nations or empires, but which I see as especially revealing of larger historical processes. Doing history from the “margins” can help us rethink how the past figures in the stories we tell about our communities. I teach at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, in a state that has itself often been treated as “marginal” in broader histories of empire, but whose history is inseparable from much wider global histories. Along with my students, I am constantly experimenting with digital storytelling, spatial humanities, and public-facing history to trace those connections between the local and the global and bring the new questions they raise to new audiences.

Much of my research asks how people in Réunion and Mauritius, French island colonies in the Indian Ocean, understood and experienced an Age of Revolutions often narrated from the North Atlantic. My first book project traces the shifting politics of race on Réunion during the era of the French and Haitian Revolutions. My work on the memoirs of a handful of globetrotting Mauritian sailors explores what it meant to call oneself “Creole” in a maritime world remade by those revolutions. My second book project documents the scope and scale of slaveholding by Catholic orders and congregations on both islands and across the wider French colonial empire. It asks, in particular, what happened to the thousands of people enslaved by missionaries when revolutionary governments declared them “national property.”

I live and work in beautiful Central Arkansas. I am originally from the Piscataqua River region of coastal New Hampshire, where I first learned the power of history to foster a meaningful sense of place.
