I am a social and cultural historian of the French colonial world, working comparatively across Creole spaces in Africa’s Indian Ocean islands, North America, and the Caribbean.

My research and teaching focus on places often dismissed as marginal to the histories of empire, but which often reveal most clearly how empires were made and maintained. Much of my research asks how people in Réunion and Mauritius, French island colonies in the Indian Ocean, understood and experienced an Age of Revolutions that is usually narrated from the North Atlantic. My first book project traces the shifting politics of race on Réunion during the era of the French and Haitian Revolutions. My work on the memoirs of a globetrotting Mauritian sailor explores what it meant to call oneself “Creole” in a maritime world remade by those revolutions. My second book project documents the scope and scale of slaveholding by Catholic orders and congregations on both islands and across the wider French colonial empire. It asks, in particular, what happened to the thousands of people enslaved by missionaries when revolutionary governments declared them “national property.”

At the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, my public-history project *Arkansas Créole* brings that perspective closer to home, recovering parts of the state’s early history that have long been overlooked or misunderstood. With my students, I use digital storytelling, spatial humanities, and public-facing history to explore the connections between local histories and wider worlds—and to carry the questions they raise beyond the classroom.

I live and work in beautiful Central Arkansas. I grew up along the Piscataqua River in coastal New Hampshire, where I first learned the power of history to foster a meaningful sense of place.
